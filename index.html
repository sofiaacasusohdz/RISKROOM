START HERE: A BEGINNER’S MAP

THE BASICS

What is an investment? What is a trade?

An investment uses capital today in pursuit of future income or appreciation. A trade is a purchase or sale of an asset, often with a shorter-term price objective. Both expose you to uncertainty.

A stock represents company ownership; a bond is a debt instrument; cash has low nominal fluctuation but can lose purchasing power through inflation. An ETF can hold many securities, although a narrowly focused ETF may remain risky.

OUR METHOD

The seven questions behind every risk decision

  1. What is my objective and time horizon?
  2. What can go wrong?
  3. How much could I lose?
  4. How are my assets connected?
  5. What limits protect my account?
  6. How will I respond to a surprise?
  7. When will I review my plan?
THE EXAMPLE

One starting account, five specialists

We will use $100,000 in fictional capital. A 60% equity / 40% bond portfolio means $60,000 is invested in shares and $40,000 in bonds. A 10% return on the first sleeve and a 2% return on the second produce a portfolio return of 6.8%: (0.60 × 10%) + (0.40 × 2%).

Percentages are weighted by the amount invested—not the number of assets.

PART 1 · IDENTIFY WHAT CAN GO WRONG

FIRST PRINCIPLES

Return is what you earn; risk is what can happen instead

Holding-period return measures the percentage change in the value of an investment, including distributions if applicable. With no dividends or fees:

Return = (Ending value − Starting value) ÷ Starting value

Buy at $100 and sell at $110: ($110 − $100)/$100 = +10%. Sell at $90 instead: −10%. Neither future outcome is certain at purchase.

Expected return is an average weighted by probabilities, not a guaranteed result. If +20% has probability 40% and −10% has probability 60%, expected return = 0.40(20%) + 0.60(−10%) = +2%.

RISK CATEGORIES

Five risks students should recognize

  • Market risk: prices fall because the overall market or sector declines.
  • Company / credit risk: a firm disappoints or a borrower cannot repay.
  • Liquidity risk: you cannot exit quickly at a reasonable price.
  • Interest-rate risk: changes in rates affect bond and other asset prices.
  • Currency and operational risk: exchange-rate movements or errors in trading systems, processes, and security.

Systematic risk affects broad markets and is difficult to diversify away. Idiosyncratic risk is asset- or company-specific and can often be reduced with diversification.

WHY THE RECOVERY FORMULA WORKS

Why −30% and +30% do not cancel

Start with $100. After a 30% loss you have $70. To recover $100, you need to earn $30 on the remaining $70, not on the original $100.

Required gain = (Original − Current) / Current = 30 / 70 = 42.86%

General form for loss fraction L: L / (1 − L). Test 50% using the slider: a portfolio halved needs +100% to get back to its starting point.

BEFORE TAKING RISK

Tolerance versus capacity

Risk tolerance describes how comfortable an investor is with uncertainty. Risk capacity describes how much loss they can financially absorb without undermining obligations. An adventurous investor who needs tuition money next month may have high tolerance but low capacity.

Ask: “Who is in a riskier situation: an investor holding volatile stocks for 25 years, or someone depending on those stocks to pay rent next week?” Discuss context, not just price swings.
WHAT PERSON 1 CAN SAY: “Risk does not mean that a loss will happen; it means a bad outcome is possible. Before choosing an investment, we must identify that outcome and decide whether we can survive it. PART 2 · BUILD A PORTFOLIO THAT CAN TAKE A HIT

TWO DIFFERENT IDEAS

Asset allocation ≠ diversification

Asset allocation decides how to split money among broad categories such as stocks, bonds and cash. Diversification spreads exposure within and across categories, industries, countries and issuers.

Holding Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon and Meta is five positions—but they may share technology and market-factor exposures. Adding assets with different drivers can reduce concentration, although no portfolio is immune to market shocks.

EXPLAIN THE FIRST CALCULATION

Where does a portfolio return come from?

Portfolio return = Σ (portfolio weight × asset return)

Suppose a $100,000 account contains $60,000 stocks and $40,000 bonds. Stocks rise 10%; bonds decline 2%.

  1. Stock contribution = 0.60 × 10% = +6.0 percentage points.
  2. Bond contribution = 0.40 × −2% = −0.8 percentage points.
  3. Total portfolio return = +5.2%, or +$5,200.

The live allocation tool uses hypothetical 9% and 3.5% annual returns. Those are assumptions for teaching, not estimates of what markets will actually earn.

EXPLAIN THE SECOND CALCULATION

Correlation and the volatility formula

Volatility (σ) is the standard deviation of returns: how spread out returns tend to be. Correlation (ρ) describes how two asset returns move together, from −1 to +1. It is calculated by dividing the covariance by the product of their standard deviations.

Portfolio variance = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂σ₁σ₂ρ

Portfolio volatility is the square root of that variance. With weights 60% stocks / 40% bonds, assumed σ of 20% / 6%, and correlation 0, variance = 0.6²×0.2² + 0.4²×0.06² = 0.014976. Its square root is 12.24%.

Move the correlation slider toward +1, then −1. Watch how the model’s risk changes without changing the amount invested.

REAL-WORLD PRACTICE

Rebalancing and hidden limitations

After strong gains, a 60/40 portfolio might become 75/25 and carry more equity risk than intended. Rebalancing restores the target weights, subject to transaction costs and taxes.

Correlations can jump during crises, bonds can decline alongside stocks, and diversified funds may still concentrate in a sector. The simple two-asset volatility formula assumes a particular historical or modeled correlation—it cannot promise safety.

Challenge: “If five investments all react the same way to the same news, how diversified are you really?”

PART 3 · CONTROL THE LOSS BEFORE OPENING A TRADE

THE TRADE PLAN

Four decisions before buying

  1. Entry: at what price would you buy?
  2. Invalidation: what price or event would tell you the idea did not work?
  3. Risk budget: how many dollars can you afford to lose?
  4. Size: how many shares fit both your risk budget and your available capital?

Do not choose the number of shares first and only afterwards decide where your stop should be.

WORKED EXAMPLE

From $100,000 to a position size

Assume a $100,000 account, a 1% risk budget per trade, a $50 entry, and a planned $45 stop.

Risk budget = 100,000 × 1% = $1,000
Planned risk per share = $50 − $45 = $5
Risk-based shares = $1,000 / $5 = 200
Capital needed = 200 × $50 = $10,000

This is 200 shares, with an expected $1,000 loss if execution occurs at $45. Actual losses can be larger because of gaps, slippage and costs. For an unleveraged long position, available cash also caps share count.

STOP-LOSS ≠ GUARANTEE

What order types can and cannot do

A stop-market order generally becomes a market order once its trigger is reached. It prioritizes exit, but may execute at a much worse price in a fast market. A stop-limit order restricts price but may never execute. Neither removes all loss risk.

Take-profit defines a price at which to realize a gain, but it does not guarantee execution under all conditions. A trade with a $5 risk per share and a $10 profit target has a planned reward-to-risk ratio of 2:1. Its actual return still depends on how often it wins, losses, fees and execution.

LEVERAGE WARNING

Why borrowing makes mistakes larger

Leverage means controlling a position larger than the capital committed. With $10,000 equity and $50,000 of exposure, leverage is 5×. A 10% adverse move in the exposure implies roughly a $5,000 loss, or 50% of equity, before borrowing costs and other effects.

Margin requirements can trigger forced liquidation; with derivatives, losses may exceed initial margin. For a beginner risk plan, it is safer to start by understanding unleveraged trades.

PEDRO CASE · STAGE 3 / CONTROL EACH TRADE

From risk budget to number of shares

Even with a diversified portfolio, one oversized trade can be dangerous. Assume Pedro plans to risk up to 1% of the $100,000 account on a specific long trade. The intended price-based risk budget is $1,000.

Risk per share = Entry price − Stop price
Shares from risk = Risk budget ÷ Risk per share
Shares permitted = floor(min(Shares from risk, Cash ÷ Entry price))

Worked example: buy at $50, stop at $45. Risk per share = $5; $1,000 ÷ $5 = 200 shares; investment = 200 × $50 = $10,000. A fill at $45 would imply a $1,000 loss, but a price gap could produce a worse exit.

Risk-reward: a planned target of $60 gives a potential gain of $10 per share versus $5 price risk: 2:1 reward-to-risk. This ratio is not a probability of success or a promise of profit.

Leverage example: a $10,000 position funded with $2,000 equity represents 5× exposure. A 10% decline in asset value is a $1,000 loss, or 50% of initial equity, ignoring interest, fees and margin liquidation.

PART 4 · MEASURE WHAT MIGHT HAPPEN

VOLATILITY VS DRAWDOWN

Two metrics that answer different questions

Volatility measures the dispersion of returns. A volatile investment can move sharply up or down. Drawdown measures the decline from a prior portfolio peak to a subsequent trough.

Drawdown = (Current portfolio value / Previous peak value) − 1

If a portfolio rises from $100,000 to $120,000 and then falls to $90,000, drawdown from the peak is −25%, not −10%. Maximum drawdown is the deepest peak-to-trough decline over the measurement period.

WHY THE VAR TOOL WORKS

Value at Risk (VaR), explained slowly

VaR estimates a loss threshold for a particular time horizon and confidence level. A one-day 95% VaR of $2,000 means that under the model, about 5% of trading days could see losses worse than $2,000. It does not mean the biggest possible loss is $2,000.

Simplified one-day VaR = Portfolio value × z × Annual volatility / √252

The displayed calculator assumes normally distributed, independent daily returns, zero expected daily return, 252 trading days and a fixed $100,000 portfolio. For 20% annual volatility and a 95% one-sided z-score around 1.645, VaR is about $2,073. This is a model-based approximation, not an observed or guaranteed result.

TAIL RISK

What VaR misses

VaR does not tell us how bad losses can be beyond the threshold. Markets may have fat tails, changing volatility, jumps and dependence that violate the simple normal model. Expected Shortfall (ES), also called Conditional VaR in some contexts, summarizes average losses in the specified worst tail.

For a complete risk review, pair numerical metrics with stress testing, liquidity limits, concentration measures and scenario analysis.

STRESS TEST

Calculate the effect of a crisis

In the simulated market crash, stocks return −30% and bonds +4%. A 60/40 portfolio therefore earns:

0.60(−30%) + 0.40(+4%) = −18% + 1.6% = −16.4%
$100,000 × (1 − 0.164) = $83,600

Now choose the rate shock: both stocks and bonds fall. Stress tests tell us how the portfolio performs under specified assumptions, not how probable the scenario is.

Ask: “Is a 95% VaR a worst-case guarantee?” Correct answer: No; the worst tail is precisely where the threshold may be exceeded.

PEDRO CASE · STAGE 4 / EVALUATE THE DOWNSIDE

Where does a 95% VaR come from?

Imagine 100 equally likely hypothetical one-day outcomes sorted from the worst loss to the best gain. For a 95% Value at Risk threshold, the left tail covers the worst 5% of modeled outcomes. VaR is a loss threshold estimated from the model, not the maximum conceivable loss.

5 red blocks = the worst 5 modeled observations. In the parametric calculator above, we assume normally distributed, zero-mean returns and scale annual volatility by √252 trading days. The one-day 95% VaR approximation is 1.645 × annual volatility ÷ √252 × portfolio value.

For example: $100,000 × 1.645 × 20% ÷ √252 ≈ $2,072. Under these assumptions, the model estimates a 5% chance of exceeding this one-day loss. Actual markets can behave differently.

Maximum drawdown: if a portfolio grows from $100,000 to $120,000 and later falls to $90,000, the peak-to-trough drawdown is ($90,000 ÷ $120,000) − 1 = −25%, even though the value is only 10% below its original starting point.

BEHAVIORAL RISKS

Why good tools still fail

FOMO means buying because others appear to be profiting; overconfidence means treating a few successful trades as proof of skill; loss aversion means feeling losses more strongly than comparable gains; and revenge trading means increasing risk impulsively after a setback.

These tendencies may cause investors to abandon their original limits just when markets become stressful.

CONCRETE EXAMPLE

The danger of doubling down

You buy $10,000 in a speculative stock. It loses 20%, leaving $8,000. You then add another $10,000 hoping to recover immediately. Your exposure is now $18,000. If the stock falls another 20% from that point, you lose a further $3,600, bringing total losses to $5,600 relative to the $20,000 committed.

Adding to an existing position is not always inappropriate, but it must be part of a pre-planned risk budget—not a reaction to embarrassment or fear.

A DISCIPLINED PROCESS

Write an investment policy before emotions rise

  1. State objective and time horizon.
  2. Set allocation and maximum concentration limits.
  3. Define position size and loss limits.
  4. Choose review dates and rebalancing rules.
  5. Record the reason for each trade in a journal.
  6. Review errors, costs and outcome without rewriting the original thesis after the fact.
DECISION PRACTICE

The portfolio drops 15%: what now?

The answer is not automatically “buy,” “sell” or “hold.” Check whether the original objective has changed, whether you exceeded a risk limit, whether the investment thesis is intact and whether you need liquidity. Then take the action dictated by the pre-established policy.

Class vote: “Should we judge a decision by whether it happened to make money, or by whether the decision followed a sound process using the information available?”

In the bull scenario, this ordering can reverse. There is no universally winning allocation.

FIVE EXPERTS, ONE RULEBOOK

What the audience should remember

  1. Identify: know what might go wrong.
  2. Allocate: diversify across meaningful risk drivers.
  3. Limit: size trades and consider liquidity and leverage.
  4. Measure: look at volatility, drawdown, VaR and stress scenarios.
  5. Execute: follow a written process and reassess as needed.

Sharpe Ratio — Was the return worth the volatility?

Absolute return alone cannot compare strategies fairly. A simple risk-adjusted measure is the Sharpe Ratio, which divides return above the risk-free rate by volatility:

Sharpe Ratio = (Portfolio return − Risk-free rate) ÷ Portfolio volatility

Example assumptions: Portfolio A earns 15% with 25% volatility; Portfolio B earns 12% with 10% volatility; risk-free rate is 4%.

Portfolio A0.44(15% − 4%) ÷ 25%
Portfolio B0.80(12% − 4%) ÷ 10%

How to interpret: B has a higher return above the risk-free rate per unit of total volatility in this example, even though A's nominal return is higher. Sharpe does not capture all risks; it can obscure tail losses and depends on the selected period and inputs.
